Step two

How we got the numbers

Every figure on this page comes from the 24 people who do the work, in their own words. There is no benchmark data here, no industry model and no estimate made on your behalf. Five steps got us from a voice note to a task-level map of the week.

01

Everyone was asked the same questions

All 24 colleagues answered by WhatsApp voice note or text, in their own words and in their own time. No workshops, no time sheets, no downtime. Most people used both voice and text.

24 of 24 responded
02

Voice notes were transcribed on our own machines

Transcription ran locally, then a correction pass fixed names, tool names and Holly & Co terms that speech models get wrong. Nothing was sent to a public transcription service.

03

Everyone got follow-up questions written for them

Each answer was read against a fixed checklist of what a complete picture needs: where work comes from and where it goes, the tools and outside parties involved, the biggest blocks of time, and what is working as well as what is not. The gaps in that person's answer became that person's follow-up questions. Nobody was asked something they had already covered.

04

Each answer became a task-by-task map of the week

Every response was broken into the tasks that person does, and each task into its component subtasks. Three things were captured against each one from their own answer: what share of their week it takes, which tools and handoffs it involves, and how they feel about doing it on a scale of minus one to plus one. Hours are that share applied to a 37.5-hour week.

221 tasks · 635 subtasks
05

Automation was assessed at subtask level, then rolled up

Each subtask was assessed for how much of it software could handle today, and those assessments roll up to the task. Assessing the small pieces rather than the whole job is what stops a task being written off as impossible because one part of it needs a person. Anything assessed at 45 per cent or above counts as automatable on this page.

How we know it holds up

  • Every number traces back to one colleague's own answer. Nothing is inferred from a job title or a benchmark.
  • The feeling reading and the automation assessment are two separate passes over the data, joined on the person and the task. All 221 tasks matched, with none dropped.
  • The totals are recomputed from the raw answers on every build and checked against the published analysis. Task count, people, hours released and percentage released all have to agree, or the build stops.

What it is not

  • Not time tracking. Hours come from each person's own account of how their week splits, not from a timer.
  • Not a commitment. An automation assessment is what software could handle, not a promise of what gets built or what it costs.
  • Not a headcount case. The output is hours released and where they go next, which is a different question from how many people you need.

The team's view, by theme

What people are saying

One comment per theme, arranged by the same themes as the message-cluster view shared earlier. Each one is a composite: it merges what several colleagues said about that theme into a single comment, so nothing here can be traced back to an individual. Open it when the room wants the words behind the numbers.

Every claim inside a composite is carried by the underlying answers, and the positives are kept in alongside the friction. Everyone answered on the understanding this was about the work, not about them, so we have written these to keep that promise. Names, job titles, team sizes and role-specific volumes are all left out, and no verbatim comment appears anywhere on this page. The individual responses stay with Tempris.

Read these as evidence, not as complaints. Every one of them points at a process, not a person. The clearest pattern across all nine themes is that people are not frustrated by the work itself, they are frustrated by the manual glue around it, and by having no time left for the part of the job they are good at.

Separate workstream

What we're building already

The product data engine, across the 28,000-product catalogue.

  • What it does. Checks each listing for the fields its category actually needs, improves weak titles and descriptions, and infers the gifting and occasion tags sellers cannot reliably set themselves. A confidence score decides what publishes, what comes to Holly & Co and what goes back to the Co.
  • Scope and phasing are agreed. Backfill and content quality first, the gifting block built into the model now but surfaced later, so limited capacity through Q4 goes to the catalogue that already exists. Tempris owns the logic and scoring; Can Factory owns the platform changes, the review queue and go-live.
  • It is pending an SoW to get started. This is the one thing on the page that is not moving. The scope, the phasing and the split of work are all settled and the team is ready, but the ten-week run at Christmas cannot begin until the statement of work is signed. Every week it waits comes off the end, not off the front.
The sequencing argument. The relief zone is the natural place to start. It is the work people like least and that software handles best, so it buys back time and goodwill at the same time. The protected zone is the opposite case and the reason to be careful: it is the largest single block of the week, people value it, and it is where the human hand Holly & Co sells actually lives.
